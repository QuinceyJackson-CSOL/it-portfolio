# Capstone: Program ePortfolio and Reflections

**Course:** University of San Diego, M.S. Cybersecurity Operations & Leadership capstone (completed in the final term of the program; M.S. conferred May 2024). I built a public ePortfolio that pulls together artifacts from across the degree, with a written reflection for each subject area.

**Skills & frameworks:** Risk management strategies (avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept/exploit), threat taxonomy, SABSA security architecture, operational policy and compliance (COPPA, duty of care, liability), software supply-chain risk, NIST RMF, professional and ethical reasoning, technical writing for mixed audiences.

**How the capstone worked:** The capstone was individual. Each module asked me to pick my strongest artifact from a past course, publish it on my ePortfolio site, and write a reflection on why I chose it and what it shows. This page covers those reflections. The detailed coursework lives on the course pages linked below.

---

## 1. Cybersecurity Fundamentals: risk strategies and common threats (Module 2)

**Objective:** Show a foundational grasp of risk types, risk responses, and the threat landscape.

**What I did:** I selected and reflected on two earlier assignments:
- **Risk management strategies.** I covered four risk types (compliance, financial, operational, strategic) and four responses: avoid, transfer, mitigate, and exploit/accept.
- **Common threats.** I covered malicious software, insider threats, hardware breaches, network spoofing, phishing, and brute-force attacks.

**Key findings / outputs:** No organization can avoid every threat, so mitigation is always part of the plan. Choosing the wrong strategy can be the difference between a well-protected system and a poorly protected one.

**Takeaways:** Every later course built on this vocabulary. When I triage an issue now, I still ask whether we should avoid, transfer, mitigate, or accept the risk.

## 2. Security Architecture (Module 3)

**Objective:** Show I could design security architecture that starts from business needs.

**What I did:** I reflected on my CSOL 520 work applying the six SABSA layers to an urgent-care medical office, a case-study scenario. That work covered physical devices, passwords, endpoint detection, and Zero Trust. The details are on the [CSOL 520 page](../csol-520-security-architecture/README.md).

**Takeaways:** Traceability, meaning each control maps back to a business requirement, is what makes an architecture defensible to auditors and leadership.

## 3. Operational Policy (Module 4)

**Objective:** Show I understood how laws, regulations, and security policy shape daily operations.

**What I did:** I selected three artifacts and reflected on each:
- **Privacy law:** the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) as an example of operational policy an organization must follow to stay compliant and avoid lawsuits.
- **Regulations and standards:** the Bangladesh Bank heist, used to explain duty of care and liability for organizations that hold customer financial data.
- **Security policy:** the SolarWinds compromise, used to show why organizations must vet who builds their software and how. A backdoor in a trusted product can be exploited long after deployment.

**Takeaways:** Compliance requirements depend on the industry: healthcare, finance, and child-serving organizations each have their own rules. Supply-chain trust is a security-policy decision, not just a purchasing one.

## 4. Risk Management (Module 4)

**Objective:** Summarize what the NIST RMF series taught me.

**What I did:** I reflected on the full RMF walkthrough. Leaders categorize by impact, set baseline controls, and then keep assessing and re-authorizing as controls are added. Continuous authorization also helps cut costs, because only the controls that support enterprise goals are kept. The details are on the [RMF page](../risk-management-nist-rmf/README.md).

**Takeaways:** Risk never goes away. New laws, workplace changes, and new controls all mean planning and assessment have to keep happening.
